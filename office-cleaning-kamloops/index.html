What Office Cleaning Covers

General cleaning for working spaces — the surfaces, floors and washrooms that get used every day.

Office cleaning is general cleaning applied to a workplace rather than a home. The work itself is familiar — wiping, dusting, floors, washrooms, emptying bins — but the rhythm is different. An office gets used by a lot of people in a short week, the mess concentrates in predictable places, and the cleaning usually has to happen around the business rather than through it.

That changes how a visit is planned. Reception and meeting rooms matter because clients see them. Staff rooms and washrooms matter because everyone uses them. Desks matter, but only the parts that can be reached without moving anyone's work. The practical scope is agreed before the first visit so there is no guessing about what gets touched.

A typical office clean can include:

  • Desks and accessible work surfaces, wiped around whatever is left out
  • Reception and entry areas, including glass on doors within reach
  • Meeting rooms and common areas — tables, chairs, visible surfaces
  • Staff rooms, break rooms and kitchenettes, including sinks, counters and the outside of appliances
  • Washrooms — toilets, sinks, mirrors, fixtures and floors, with supplies restocked if you provide them
  • Hard floors swept and mopped; carpeted areas vacuumed
  • High-touch points such as door handles, light switches, railings and shared surfaces
  • Rubbish and recycling emptied into the bins or location you nominate
  • General dusting of ledges, sills and reachable fixtures

Two things we deliberately leave alone. We do not move or sort through employee belongings, paperwork or anything left on a desk — it gets cleaned around, not relocated. And we do not clean the inside of computers, servers, phones, printers, lab equipment or anything else where a cleaning cloth could do damage. Screens and keyboards can be included if you ask for it and accept that it is a light surface wipe.

This is general cleaning, not specialist disinfection. If your workplace needs certified sanitisation, clean-room protocols or anything medically regulated, that is a different trade and you should hire for it specifically.

Recurring Office Cleaning

The arrangement most offices end up on, and the one our commercial rate is built around.

Most workplaces are better served by a schedule than by occasional deep cleans. An office that is cleaned on a regular cycle never accumulates the kind of buildup that takes hours to undo, which keeps each visit short and the cost predictable. It also means the cleaner learns the space — where the bins are, which rooms are in use, which door is locked after five — and stops needing to be told.

Schedules are set around how the space is actually used. A small professional office with a handful of staff may only need a weekly visit. A busier space with client traffic, a shared kitchen and two washrooms often works better on two or three visits a week. Some businesses want only the washrooms and common areas covered frequently and the rest less often. All of that is worked out before anything is booked.

Recurring commercial and janitorial cleaning is charged at $45–$50 per cleaner/hour, depending on the scope and the cleaning schedule. Where a job sits in that range comes down to a few things:

  • Size of the space and how many rooms are in scope
  • Scope per visit — washrooms and kitchens take longer than open desk areas
  • Frequency — a space cleaned weekly needs less work per visit than one cleaned monthly
  • Specific requirements such as after-hours access, restocking, or particular floor care

We quote the hourly rate and an expected visit length, so you can see the likely cost per visit before committing to anything. If a space consistently takes less time than estimated, the schedule gets adjusted rather than the hours being padded.

One-Time Office & Commercial Cleaning

Single jobs are quoted on the space and the scope rather than an hourly list price.

Not every commercial job belongs on a schedule. One-time work varies far more than recurring work does — two offices of the same floor area can need very different amounts of time depending on what has happened in them. For that reason single jobs are quoted individually rather than published at a fixed rate.

Common one-time requests include:

  • An office reset — a space that has not had a proper clean in a while and needs to be brought back to a reasonable baseline
  • Moving in or out of a commercial unit — cleaning an empty space before you take it over, or clearing it to hand back
  • A pre-opening clean — getting a new or refitted space presentable before the doors open
  • Post-event cleanup — a workspace used for an event, training day or client function

Send the details and we will come back with a price for the job. A photo or two of the space is usually enough to confirm the scope without arranging a visit, which is the fastest route to an accurate number. For a commercial unit being handed back, mention any condition requirements in your lease so the scope is agreed up front rather than renegotiated afterwards.

If a renovation or fit-out has just finished, the dust from that work is its own job — see post-renovation cleaning, which is quoted the same way.

What Office Cleaning Does Not Cover

Worth stating plainly so nobody books the wrong trade.

Office and commercial cleaning here means general cleaning of a workspace. It is not specialist industrial or restoration work, and we will tell you so rather than take a job we are not the right fit for. We do not handle:

  • Hazardous material or asbestos of any kind
  • Demolition, or clearing construction debris as a trade
  • Fire or flood restoration
  • Biohazard remediation or certified decontamination
  • Specialist industrial cleanup, plant or machinery cleaning

If a job involves any of that, you need a specialist contractor for that part. Once the specialist work is finished and the space is safe, the general clean afterwards is something we can quote.

We also do not move employee belongings or clean the interior of equipment — see the scope notes above.
